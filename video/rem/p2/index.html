The Middle East has oil. China has rare earths.DENG XIAOPING · AS REPORTED · 1992
And more than eight out of every ten come from one country.
China.
So you're probably thinking —
if the sand is ours,
why can't we just dig it up and make them?
Let's open that box.
Here's the catch, and it may surprise you.
Rare earths are not actually rare.
The rare part is getting them out clean.
There are 17 of them.
And when you dig them up,
you get them mixed together.
Think of them as 17 brothers and sisters who look almost exactly alike.
Nearly the same size, same habits,
almost the same chemistry.
To your eye, and to most chemicals,
they look the same.
So how do you pull one of them out of the crowd?
There's no single magic step.
You dissolve the mix in acid,
and the liquid flows through a long row of tanks.
Each tank pulls them apart... only a tiny bit.
So you repeat it.
Again and again.
A big plant can run hundreds of these tanks in a row,
just to get one element clean.
Think of it like climbing stairs.
Each tank is one step... it lifts you only a little.
Want one rare earth, nearly pure?
You climb hundreds of steps.
And that's only the first half.
The second half is faster.
Let me run you through it.
Next, the clean rare earth becomes a metal.
You melt it with iron and boron... and that mix is called an alloy.
Then you cool it into thin pieces.
Hydrogen gas cracks those pieces,
and you grind them into a powder finer than flour.
You line that powder up inside a strong magnetic field,
press it into a block,
and bake it at about 1,000 degrees Celsius.
Only at the very end do you switch on its magnetism.
That's when you finally have a magnet.
So a magnet isn't one factory.
It's a chain.
Sand, to mixed rare earths,
to clean rare earth powder, to metal, to alloy,
to magnet.
Miss one link... and you're stuck.
Remember this chain.
It's the whole story.
So how did China end up leading every link?
In 1992,
China's leader Deng Xiaoping is reported to have said
— the Middle East has oil,
China has rare earths.
