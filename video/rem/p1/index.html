July, 2025.
Pune.
Bajaj's electric Chetak factory is making barely half the scooters it made a year earlier.
The reason?
Not a strike.
Not batteries.
One small magnet... and most of them come from China.
A year earlier,
about 20,000 Chetaks a month.
This July... less than 11,000.
And the crazy part?
The raw material for that magnet is lying in the sand on our own beaches
— in Andhra Pradesh, Kerala,
Tamil Nadu and Odisha.
By our own government's count,
India's sands hold about 7 million tonnes of rare earths.
So why can't India make the magnet?
Stay with me, and you'll see three things.
The hard step nobody else wanted to do.
Then, about five minutes in,
the secret inside our sand that kept it locked away.
And at the end, the date you should watch.
First, what is this magnet?
It's called a rare earth magnet.
Mostly iron, a pinch of boron,
and a metal called neodymium.
It's small, but it's very strong.
And in a motor, strength is everything.
Here's why.
A stronger magnet gives you the same push from a smaller motor.
Smaller means lighter.
And lighter means you get more kilometres on one charge.
Makes sense?
That's why most electric scooters and cars you see on the road use these magnets in their motors.
And not just vehicles.
The same kind of magnet sits in your phone's little vibration motor,
in many wind turbines, and in defence systems.
Now look at this number.
India needs about 4,000 tonnes of these magnets a year... and we buy almost all of them from abroad.
And more than eight out of every ten come from one country.
China.
So you're probably thinking —
if the sand is ours,
why can't we just dig it up and make them?
Let's open that box.
Here's the catch, and it may surprise you.
Rare earths are not actually rare.
The rare part is getting them out clean.
There are 17 of them.
And when you dig them up,
you get them mixed together.
