RIS DP 268
Penicillin is different.
Penicillin is grown.
You take a special mould... a cousin of the green patches you've seen on old bread.
You put it in a giant steel tank,
and you feed it sugar and air.
For about a week, the tank runs day and night.
The mould eats, grows,
and gives out penicillin into the liquid.
And if even one stray germ gets in,
you can lose the whole batch.
Then you take the penicillin out and clean it.
This first powder is called penicillin G.
Next, it's turned into a smaller base powder.
Factories call it six A.P.A.,
but let's just call it the base.
From that base, factories make amoxicillin —
the antibiotic in so many strips at home.
Now here's the secret of this business.
A bigger tank makes the powder cheaper.
Much cheaper.
Why?
The tank must stay perfectly clean, cool,
and full of air, every hour of every day.
That takes a lot of power, water and steam.
Think of a flour mill.
A big chakki grinds each kilo cheaper than a small one.
Double your tank,
and your costs don't double... but your output does.
So you can guess who wins.
The country with the biggest tanks and the cheapest power.
And that's exactly where India's story turns.
Now, here's what we got wrong.
For years,
India's government allowed only a few companies to make penicillin,
and only in small amounts.
So our plants stayed too small to be cheap.
Meanwhile, in the nineteen nineties,
China went all in on medicine ingredients.
It built huge plants, in huge drug parks,
with cheap power and cheap water.
Its factories also faced looser pollution rules.
And then cheap Chinese penicillin flooded the world market... at prices nobody else could match.
Now remember this one number.
Nineteen ninety-one —
the year India opened up its economy.
Of all the medicine powder India bought from outside,
China sold less than one per cent.
Less than one.
