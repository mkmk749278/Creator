# Plan · India Had a Catapult in 1961 (carrier-catapult) · v2, 2026-11-12 (after the independent review, `review_plan_v1.md`)

## The promise (**locked: the owner approved plan v2, the title and the thumbnail concept on 2026-11-12**)
**Promise in one sentence:** India threw jets off a ship with a catapult in 1961 and fought the 1971 war with it;
in 2026 its newest carrier has a ramp, and the Rafale it has bought was built to be thrown. You'll see what a
catapult really has to do in three seconds, why India gave it up, why even America is arguing about it, and what
India has actually built so far.

**Title (main):** **India Had a Catapult in 1961. Why Not Now?** (42 characters)
- Alt 1 (name + number + contradiction): 26 Rafales Built for a Catapult India Lacks (43)
- Alt 2 (the search words): Why INS Vikrant Has a Ramp, Not a Catapult (41)
*Why:* "had it, but doesn't now" is the form that travelled (§1.2), and here it is a fact with a date. The roadmap's
"Why India Can't Build…" is not sourced: India has not tried at full size and failed. Nobody searches "ski jump";
they search Rafale, INS Vikrant and catapult (`research/search_terms.md`), so those words carry the package.

**Thumbnail concept (main): "HAD ONE. 1961."**: bright sea daylight, warm low sun. The Rafale M on INS Vikrant's
deck, nose toward the ramp (the Navy's own 29 October photo if the licence and the angle allow, otherwise our
Blender render from it). Under the jet, where a catapult track would run, a thick, solid glowing yellow band is set
into the deck, and it stops dead at the foot of the ramp. Words, upper left, caution yellow on a dark band:
**HAD ONE. 1961.** Bottom-right empty. Visual question: *had what? what is that missing line?* The title asks, the
picture shows today, the words say the past: each carries a different half (the reviewer's point; "NO CATAPULT"
repeated the title's noun and read as a taunt).
- Challenger 1: **NO CATAPULT**: the same picture, the band redder, the words blunt.
- Challenger 2: **3 SECONDS**: the deck cut open in light, a line of coils glowing under the same jet (the bright,
  plain-object version; scored low for recognition, kept only as the Test & Compare outlier).
- Not used: a 1961/2026 split (5/10 on a phone); "INDIA CAN'T" (not true as said); a Fujian comparison (wins the
  mockers' click, §1.2); the ₹ number (it is the film's reward, not its door).
**Sentence 1 (promise check, draft):** "On the twenty-ninth of October, a Rafale came down on the deck of
I-N-S Vikrant. This jet was built to be thrown off a ship by a catapult. Vikrant has no catapult. It has a ramp.
The Rafale can leave by that ramp, it passed that test in Goa. But here is the strange part. India's first carrier,
in nineteen sixty-one, had a catapult."
It confirms all three: the jet, the ramp and 1961 (HAD ONE. 1961.), the missing catapult (NO CATAPULT), and the
throw (3 SECONDS, paid at 0:30). It never says the jet cannot fly from Vikrant.

**Edge:** everyone argues about which carrier India should build. Nobody shows the Indian viewer what a catapult
actually is: under two hundred rupees of electricity that must be delivered in three seconds, a problem of power,
not energy. And nobody remembers that India once kept a catapult alive with steel bands and went to war with it.

**Honesty rules for this film:**
- Never "India can't build". Say what exists (a 400 kg demonstrator), what is needed (30 to 40 tonnes), and what is
  missing (a ship, a power plant, a decision).
- The ramp's payload penalty has never been published. Say so. No invented tonnes. The certain loss is the radar
  aircraft.
- The 2017 US "offer" was reported, never signed: "reports said".
- EMALS is neither a failure nor finished: both numbers together (614 against 4,166; 36,863 launches).
- China is said plainly, once, without envy or mockery. Pakistan is not in this film.
- The rupee and megawatt figures carry OUR CALCULATION **with the working on screen**, and the rupee figure is never
  said without the three seconds beside it (alone it invites "so cheap, why can't we?").
- The Rafale M was chosen after ramp trials at Goa and can fly from Vikrant. The film never implies otherwise.
- The $1.3 billion is the ceiling of a US sale approval that also covered arresting gear: said so.
- 1971 is told with dignity: no enemy-bashing, the engineers are the heroes.

**Length:** about 12 and a half minutes (ch1 cut to 75 s so 1961 arrives by 2:00). **Feeling curve:** puzzled pride (open) → unease (ch1) → warmth and a
lump in the throat (ch2) → wonder (ch3) → doubt (ch4) → sober honesty, the low point (ch5) → steady resolve
(landing). No two neighbours share a feeling.

## 2. Cold opens considered
The obvious one, set aside: a montage of carrier launches with "aircraft carriers are floating airfields".
1. **The landing (chosen): one moment.** The Navy's real footage, 29 October: the hook catches, the jet stops.
   Sentence 1. Then the camera travels up the deck to the ramp, and the glowing slot from the thumbnail draws
   itself and stops. *Why:* first frame = the thumbnail's subject, in a real place with real Indian sailors; it is
   the news the viewer already saw, turned into a question.
2. **A sound.** Black screen, three seconds of a launch heard from one deck below (a real recording exists), then
   "that was all of it". Strong for B, but the first frame would be a void and the thumbnail's jet would be missing.
3. **The viewer's own home.** "Your home used about this much electricity in the last week." Then the meter
   empties in three seconds. A true surprise, but it gives away the film's reward in sentence 1 (§5.1: confirm,
   never answer).
4. **The failure.** 1970, Bombay: a crack in a boiler drum, and the catapult goes cold. Only this story has it, but
   a stranger does not yet know why a catapult matters.

## 3. Endings considered
1. **The same deck, the line drawn through (chosen).** Back on Vikrant's deck from the opening. The glowing slot
   returns and this time runs on, off the screen, onto a ship that is not built: "The jet is bought. The motor is
   four hundred kilos old. The ship is not yet approved." The saying. The dates to watch. *Why:* it pays the
   thumbnail's missing line and leaves the caring viewer with who, what and when.
2. **The steel band.** End on Roy Chowdhury's boiler: "India has done harder things with less." Warm, but it ends
   on the past, and close to chest-thumping.
3. **The next three seconds.** End on DRDO's sled leaving its trailer in real time, and silence. Honest, but small
   and cold for a landing.

  moment, the viewer's own objection answered straight. Landing: the bookend.
- **The saying to take home:** **"You can buy the jet. You have to build the throw."** (The reviewer's pick. "A
  carrier is only as strong as its first three seconds" overclaims; "the ramp asks the jet to do the work" is wrong,
  because a catapult jet also runs its engines flat out.)
- **The retell line:** "The electricity to throw a fighter jet off a ship costs under two hundred rupees. The hard
  part is giving it in three seconds. And India ran a catapult in 1971 with steel bands round a cracked boiler."
- **The memory every Indian shares:** December 1971.
- **Art direction:** sea daylight and warm brass, not the navy-black of defence channels. 1961-71 in warm
  monochrome archive on a drafting table with the official history's pages; the present in real Navy footage; the
  mechanism as cutaways lit from inside on a steel inspection plate. **Sound palette:** deck wind, the three-second
  motif, a pressure cooker's whistle (ch2 only), a low tanpura-like drone under 1971, silence at the count.
- **Why ours beats the strongest three:** Not What You Think explains the American machine with jokes and no
  stakes for us; the Hindi INS Vishal films promise a ship with no budget; the news clips show the landing and stop.
  Ours is the only one that answers "why the ramp", from India, with a number a family can repeat.

## 8a. Fact-check results (step 4, 2026-11-12): `claims.csv` passes the gate (24 confirmed, 13 said as reported, 2 our calculation)
What changed in the plan, and what stays open:
- **The energy store:** "four disks of 121 MJ" is the 1995 design paper, not the machine that was built. General
  Atomics' own figure: a motor generator of 80,000 lb giving 60 MJ at 60 MW. The film says "heavy spinning machines,
  each over 36 tonnes" and gives no count.
- **The home comparison:** an average Indian home uses 98 units a month (Data For India, from the household survey),
  so a 30-tonne jet's 19 units is about **six days**, and roughly ₹120 at about ₹6.5 a unit: "under two hundred
  rupees" holds. The "town" comparison is dropped (no sourced input). Peak about 50 MW against Vikrant's 88 MW (GE).
- **1971:** the cracked drums, "not feasible indigenously" and 14 knots stand on the official-history text (two
  sources); the steel bands and safety valves on two (Karve's account from the official history; Republic World,
  2023). Not said: the number of Sea Hawks and the hour on 4 December (six at 3 pm or eight in the morning), and
  "first" or "only" for the Vir Chakra. New and usable: his medal was presented to INS Shivaji, Lonavala, on
  18 December 2023; at 12 knots only the Alizés could fly, and a relit boiler brought the Sea Hawks back (one source).
- **The catapult came out in 1989**, when the Alizés retired, not simply "1987-89".
- **China:** it did build a steam test catapult on land (a pair, one of each, at Huangdicun from 2016), so "never
  built steam" became "never sent a steam catapult to sea".
- **DRDO:** no DRDO page, newsletter or press note on the launcher could be found (Cowork searched drdo.gov.in; the
  Technology Focus issue is about the railgun). The 400 kg and 16 to 18 m come from R&DE(E)'s statement as carried by
  India TV's reporter and idrw (Aug 2024); "a decade or more" from one outlet's unnamed officials. All said as
  reported. **This is the film's weakest sourcing and the narration is worded for it.**
- **The US:** the memo's own words are in hand (a plan, for CVN-81); "nearly 50% of production complete" is General
  Atomics' statement; DOT&E FY2025 is in hand. 181 (2020), 614 (2022), 4,166 required: confirmed. 36,863 launches is
  the programme's own count, attributed.
