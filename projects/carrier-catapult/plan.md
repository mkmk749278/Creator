# Plan · India Had a Catapult in 1961 (carrier-catapult) · v1, 2026-11-12

## The promise (proposed; locked when the owner approves this plan)
**Promise in one sentence:** India threw jets off a ship with a catapult in 1961 and fought the 1971 war with it;
in 2026 its newest carrier has a ramp, and the Rafale it has bought was built to be thrown. You'll see what a
catapult really has to do in three seconds, why India gave it up, why even America is arguing about it, and what
India has actually built so far.

**Title (main):** **India Had a Catapult in 1961. Why Not Now?** (42 characters)
- Alt 1 (name + number + contradiction): 26 Rafales Built for a Catapult India Lacks (43)
- Alt 2 (the search words): Why INS Vikrant Has a Ramp, Not a Catapult (41)
*Why:* "had it, but doesn't now" is the form that travelled (§1.2), and here it is a fact with a date. The roadmap's
"Why India Can't Build…" is not sourced: India has not tried at full size and failed. Nobody searches "ski jump";
they search Rafale, INS Vikrant and catapult (`research/search_terms.md`), so those words carry the package.

**Thumbnail concept (main): "NO CATAPULT"**: bright sea daylight, warm low sun. The Rafale M on INS Vikrant's
deck, nose toward the ramp (the Navy's own 29 October photo if the licence and the angle allow, otherwise our
Blender render from it). Under the jet, where a catapult track would run, a glowing yellow dashed slot is drawn into
the deck, and it stops dead at the foot of the ramp. Words, upper left, caution yellow on a dark band:
**NO CATAPULT**. Bottom-right empty. Visual question: *what is that missing line, and how does this jet leave?*
- Challenger 1: **1961 ✓ 2026 ✗** as picture, words **INDIA HAD ONE**: left half a Sea Hawk on the old Vikrant's
  catapult in warm black-and-white, right half the Rafale at the ramp in colour.
- Challenger 2: **3 SECONDS**: the deck cut open in light, a line of coils glowing under a jet (the bright,
  plain-object version).
- Not used: "INDIA CAN'T" (not true as said); a Fujian comparison (wins the mockers' click, §1.2); the ₹200 number
  (it is the film's reward, not its door).
**Sentence 1 (promise check, draft):** "On the twenty-ninth of October, a Rafale came down on the deck of
I-N-S Vikrant. This jet was built to be thrown off a ship by a catapult. Vikrant has no catapult. It has a ramp.
And here is the strange part. India's first carrier, in nineteen sixty-one, had one."
It confirms all three: the jet, the ramp and the missing catapult (NO CATAPULT), 1961 (INDIA HAD ONE), and the
throw (3 SECONDS is paid at 0:30).

**Edge:** everyone argues about which carrier India should build. Nobody shows the Indian viewer what a catapult
actually is: about two hundred rupees of electricity that must be delivered in three seconds, a problem of power,
not energy. And nobody remembers that India once kept a catapult alive with steel bands and went to war with it.

**Honesty rules for this film:**
- Never "India can't build". Say what exists (a 400 kg demonstrator), what is needed (30 to 40 tonnes), and what is
  missing (a ship, a power plant, a decision).
- The ramp's payload penalty has never been published. Say so. No invented tonnes. The certain loss is the radar
  aircraft.
- The 2017 US "offer" was reported, never signed: "reports said".
- EMALS is neither a failure nor finished: both numbers together (614 against 4,166; 36,863 launches).
- China is said plainly, once, without envy or mockery. Pakistan is not in this film.
- The rupee and megawatt figures carry OUR CALCULATION on screen. The lift story is "as reported" or it is cut.
- 1971 is told with dignity: no enemy-bashing, the engineers are the heroes.

**Length:** about 12 and a half minutes. **Feeling curve:** puzzled pride (open) → unease (ch1) → warmth and a
lump in the throat (ch2) → wonder (ch3) → doubt (ch4) → sober honesty, the low point (ch5) → steady resolve
(landing). No two neighbours share a feeling.

## 2. Cold opens considered
The obvious one, set aside: a montage of carrier launches with "aircraft carriers are floating airfields".
1. **The landing (chosen): one moment.** The Navy's real footage, 29 October: the hook catches, the jet stops.
   Sentence 1. Then the camera travels up the deck to the ramp, and the glowing slot from the thumbnail draws
   itself and stops. *Why:* first frame = the thumbnail's subject, in a real place with real Indian sailors; it is
   the news the viewer already saw, turned into a question.
2. **A sound.** Black screen, three seconds of a launch heard from one deck below (a real recording exists), then
   "that was all of it". Strong for B, but the first frame would be a void and the thumbnail's jet would be missing.
3. **The viewer's own home.** "Your home used about this much electricity in the last ten days." Then the meter
   empties in three seconds. A true surprise, but it gives away the film's reward in sentence 1 (§5.1: confirm,
   never answer).
4. **The failure.** 1970, Bombay: a crack in a boiler drum, and the catapult goes cold. Only this story has it, but
   a stranger does not yet know why a catapult matters.

## 3. Endings considered
1. **The same deck, the line drawn through (chosen).** Back on Vikrant's deck from the opening. The glowing slot
   returns and this time runs on, off the screen, onto a ship that is not built: "The jet is bought. The motor is
   four hundred kilos old. The ship is not yet approved." The saying. The dates to watch. *Why:* it pays the
   thumbnail's missing line and leaves the caring viewer with who, what and when.
2. **The steel band.** End on Roy Chowdhury's boiler: "India has done harder things with less." Warm, but it ends
   on the past, and close to chest-thumping.
3. **The next three seconds.** End on DRDO's sled leaving its trailer in real time, and silence. Honest, but small
   and cold for a landing.

## 4. The creative spine
- **The one image:** an ordinary Indian home electricity meter. Ten days of its units run out in three seconds, and
  a 30-tonne jet is gone. (Sized from the real numbers; OUR CALCULATION.)
- **The motif (picture):** the glowing slot in the deck. (1) 0:20 it draws and stops at the ramp: the absence.
  (2) ch2, 1961: the real slot, steam rising from it. (3) ch3: we go down into it: coils. (4) ch5: 17 metres of it
  on a trailer in Pune, laid beside the 91 it must become. (5) landing: it runs on, to a ship not built.
- **The motif (sound):** three seconds. A rising electric whine, a hard thump, then open wind. Heard five times:
  0:30 (unexplained), ch2 as steam hiss and a clang, ch3 in full with a spoken count, ch5 small and thin (the
  400 kg sled), landing: the whine begins and the film cuts to silence before the thump.
- **One device per chapter:** open: the news you already saw, turned round. Ch1: the viewer placed on the deck
  ("stand here with me"). Ch2: one object followed through (the boiler drum, 1961 to 1989). Ch3: a real-time count
  of three seconds. Ch4: a reversal (the inventor doubts, the newcomer leaps). Ch5: a confession ("let me be honest
  with you") and the Kishore moment. Landing: the bookend.
- **The saying to take home (final wording in narration; three candidates):** "A carrier is only as strong as its
  first three seconds." / "The ramp asks the jet to do the work. The catapult asks the ship." / "You can buy the
  jet. You have to build the throw."
- **The retell line:** "The electricity to throw a fighter jet off a ship costs about two hundred rupees. The hard
  part is giving it in three seconds. And India ran a catapult in 1971 with steel bands round a cracked boiler."
- **The memory every Indian shares:** December 1971.
- **Art direction:** sea daylight and warm brass, not the navy-black of defence channels. 1961-71 in warm
  monochrome archive on a drafting table with the official history's pages; the present in real Navy footage; the
  mechanism as cutaways lit from inside on a steel inspection plate. **Sound palette:** deck wind, the three-second
  motif, a pressure cooker's whistle (ch2 only), a low tanpura-like drone under 1971, silence at the count.
- **Why ours beats the strongest three:** Not What You Think explains the American machine with jokes and no
  stakes for us; the Hindi INS Vishal films promise a ship with no budget; the news clips show the landing and stop.
  Ours is the only one that answers "why the ramp", from India, with a number a family can repeat.
