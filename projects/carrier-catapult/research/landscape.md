## 1. What everyone is saying
- **"India's supercarrier INS Vishal will have EMALS"** has been a headline since 2015 (the India-US Joint Working
  Group on Aircraft Carrier Technology Cooperation, January 2015; reports in October 2017 that the US agreed to
  release EMALS to India). Nine years of videos promise it; no ship has been approved.
- **"Rafale Marine lands on INS Vikrant"** (29 October 2026, first attempt): the freshest news, covered as a triumph.
  Almost nobody adds that the Rafale M was designed for a catapult and Vikrant has a ski-jump, or that Vikrant's
  lifts are 10 m wide and the jet's fixed wing is 10.9 m (militaeraktuell.at).
- **"China's Fujian has electromagnetic catapults"** (commissioned 5 November 2025, conventionally powered; China
  skipped steam). Used in India mostly for India-vs-China comparison clips.
- **"Trump orders the US Navy back to steam"** (memorandum of 13 August 2026: a plan within 60 days to replace EMALS
  with steam on CVN-81). The biggest English film on it: Not What You Think, "Why the US Navy is Replacing Electric
  Catapults with Steam" (January 2026, 11 min).
- **YouTube, India (channel API search, 12 Nov 2026):** "why India aircraft carrier ski jump" returns almost only
  Shorts of 11 to 47 seconds; the Indian long films are INS Vishal wish-lists (2016 to 2025, 5 to 10 min) and Hindi
  explainers. Nobody has made the plain Indian film on *why the ramp, what a catapult needs, and where India stands*.

## 2. What it actually is (draft)
**The machine.** EMALS is a linear induction motor: the stator coils of an ordinary motor laid out flat in a line.
Only the coils around the moving carriage are energised. The motor is about 91 m (300 ft) long and can take a 45-tonne
aircraft to about 240 km/h. The ship cannot supply that burst directly, so it charges four disk alternators for about
45 seconds; each stores 121 MJ as spin at up to 6,400 rpm, slowing to about 5,205 rpm in a maximum launch; the energy
goes out in 2 to 3 seconds. One motor generator weighs over 36 tonnes (80,000 lb). A steam catapult delivers about
95 MJ and uses about 610 kg of steam a launch. *(secondary: Wikipedia and mirrors quoting the Navy's design paper,
Doyle et al., IEEE Trans. Magnetics 31(1), 1995; the paper itself is still to read.)*

**Why the ramp.** A ski-jump has no moving parts; the jet leaves on its own engines, in afterburner. So only
high-thrust fighters can use it, and a heavy propeller aircraft with a radar dish (the E-2 Hawkeye kind) cannot.
India's carriers therefore use Ka-31 helicopters as their radar eyes; India looked at the Hawkeye in the early 2000s
and dropped it (Livefist; *secondary*). Vikrant's hull has no space under the ramp for a catapult (analysis, not a
Navy statement).

**Where India stands.**
- DRDO's R&DE(E), Pune: a scaled demonstrator that throws up to 400 kg over 16 to 18 m (idrw, Aug 2024); shown on a
  trailer at Aero India 2025, being tested with UAVs up to 500 kg; the Navy wants 30 tonnes, the full system is
  aimed at 40; DRDO officials: "a decade or more" (idrw, Apr 2025). *(secondary; DRDO's own pages still to find.)*
- The MoD's 15-year roadmap (TPCR 2025, September 2025) asks for **two EMALS** and arresting gear for a future
  carrier, and lists nuclear propulsion; "DRDO is currently developing EMALS for this requirement" (Naval News).
- The carrier actually close to approval is a **repeat Vikrant** of about 45,000 tonnes, ski-jump again, with changes
  for drones; in-principle nod in September 2024, the Navy hoped for clearance in 2026 (idrw, Indian Defence News;
  one French report says CATOBAR; **unsettled, re-check**).
- The US said yes: export approval for EMALS and arresting gear reported in 2017; France's possible buy of one
  two-launcher EMALS and arresting gear was put at $1.321 billion (US State Department notice, Dec 2021).
- The jets: 26 Rafale M ordered 28 April 2025 for about ₹63,000 crore, deliveries from about 2028 to 2030-31.

**The hard truth about EMALS itself.** USS Ford's EMALS managed 181 mean cycles between operational mission failures
in 2019-20 and 614 in March to June 2022 against a requirement of 4,166 (DOT&E via CRS); the FY2025 DOT&E report says
EMALS and arresting-gear reliability still limits sortie generation. Yet Ford had logged 36,863 electromagnetic
launches by March 2026 and over 12,200 in its 326-day deployment (Navy Lookout). The UK looked at fitting catapults
to a Queen Elizabeth carrier in 2010-12; the estimate went from about £800 million to about £2 billion and it was
dropped.

**The memory.** INS Vikrant (1961), bought from Britain, had a steam catapult and threw Sea Hawks and Alizés; her
aircraft struck Chittagong and Cox's Bazar in December 1971. In June 1970 a boiler drum feeding the catapult had
cracked, no replacement could be had, and Admiral S. M. Nanda had steam routed from the forward machinery
*(single source, fan wiki: verify in Hiranandani's official history)*. The catapult came out and a ski-jump went on
in the 1987-89 refit for the Sea Harriers.

## 3. Our edge (draft, one sentence)
Everyone argues about *which* carrier India should build; nobody shows the Indian viewer the one physical fact under
the argument: **a catapult is a three-second power cut the size of a city, and India has to learn to store it, throw
it and repeat it every 45 seconds, a job even its inventor has not finished**, while the jet built for that throw
has just landed on India's ramp.

Possible angles for the plan (differ in kind): (A) the 1961 catapult India gave up; (B) the three-second energy
problem as the spine; (C) the Rafale M on the ramp: what the jet leaves behind on the deck; (D) the missing eyes:
no catapult, no radar aircraft; (E) three navies, three bets (America doubts, China leaps, India waits).

## 5. The objection ledger (draft; from what the strongest films' viewers will say, comments still to read)
1. "Ski-jump is cheaper and good enough; catapults are a rich navy's toy." What the sources say: no moving parts and
   lower cost are true; the price is paid in fuel, weapons and the radar aircraft.
2. "Just buy EMALS from America, it was offered." True since 2017; the cost (France: about $1.3 billion for one
   system), the carrier to put it in, and the power plant are the missing parts.
3. "Even America is going back to steam, so EMALS is a failure." The reliability numbers are bad against the
   requirement, and Ford still made tens of thousands of launches; the order is for a plan, on one ship.
4. "China did it, so why can't India?" China spent years on land test tracks and chose it at the top; India's
   demonstrator is at 400 kg.
5. "Carriers are sitting ducks; spend on submarines." Outside this film; one honest line, then on.

**1970-71, now from the Navy's own historian** (Live History India quoting Vice Admiral G. M. Hiranandani's
*Transition to Triumph*): Vikrant "had suffered many internal fatigue cracks and fissures in the water drums of her
boilers"; docked at Bombay's Naval Dockyard in June 1970; Hiranandani to Admiral Nanda: "Vikrant had to be seen
being operational even if we didn't fly any aircraft", and "During the 1965 war, Vikrant was sitting in Bombay
Harbour and did not go out to sea"; she ran at 14 knots, about half her 25; on 4 December 1971, about 3 pm, six Sea
Hawks left her deck, "almost skimming the surface of the sea". The embargo and the re-routed steam stay unverified
(dropped unless the book confirms them). *Still open: how a steam catapult worked on half-pressure boilers, which is
the heart of the scene; the book itself.*

**China** (MP-IDSA issue brief, A. K. Darbey, 17 Dec 2025): Fujian is about 80,000 tons with three electromagnetic
catapults and a flat deck; its catapult runs on a DC power system (America's on AC), after Ma Weiming's
medium-voltage DC design; the KJ-600 fixed-wing radar aircraft launches from it; sea trials from May 2024, eight
rounds by August 2025.

**India's next carrier:** no approval reported by 12 Nov 2026. Admiral Dinesh Tripathi (30 May 2026, a day before
retiring): carriers "remain central to maritime power projection". Reports differ on whether the repeat Vikrant
would be a third carrier or replace Vikramaditya.

**The US plan:** due mid-October 2026; nothing published by 12 Nov. AP: the switch "could cost billions"; General
Atomics: equipment for that ship nearly 50% complete; a Hudson Institute analyst: the Navy has not bought a new
steam catapult in more than 20 years.
