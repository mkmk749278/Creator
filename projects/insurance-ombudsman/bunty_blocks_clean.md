Now listen carefully. You cannot jump straight to the Ombudsman on the day your claim is rejected. If you do, your complaint can be turned away at the door. In twenty twenty-four to twenty-five alone, seventeen hundred and fifteen health complaints were turned away for this one reason. The person had not complained to the insurance company first. Under Rule fourteen, you must follow a two-step clock. Clock step one. This is step two of our plan. Complain in writing to the insurance company, best of all to its Grievance Redressal Officer, the G-R-O. Every insurer has to show its grievance officer's details on its website, and the Ombudsman's website lists their email addresses too. Send one calm, formal email with your policy number, your claim number, and why their deduction is wrong. Keep a copy of that email and every reply. You will need them later. Under the I-R-D-A-I rules, the company should resolve your grievance within fourteen days. Clock step two. Wait for their reply, or wait one month. The moment the company rejects your complaint, or you are not happy with its reply, or one month passes with no reply at all... the door to the Ombudsman opens. From that day, you have one year to file your complaint with the Ombudsman, for compensation of up to fifty lakh rupees. That limit was raised to fifty lakh rupees in November twenty twenty-three. And remember one strict condition. The Ombudsman cannot take your complaint if the same dispute is already pending, or already decided, in any court, consumer forum, or arbitration.

So, how do you actually file, and what happens next? Open your browser and go to the official website, c-i-o-i-n-s dot co dot in. Choose Register Complaint, and verify your mobile number with an O-T-P. Then you choose your Ombudsman office. There are eighteen offices across India, in cities like Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata. You pick the one that covers the place where you live, or the insurance company's branch that handled your policy. Before you submit, keep these documents ready. One. A copy of your insurance policy. Two. The hospital discharge summary and the itemised final bill. Three. The company's rejection or deduction letter. Four. A copy of the complaint you sent to the insurance company. And five. Your I-D and address proof, and a photograph. Each file can be a P-D-F or a J-P-G, up to ten M-B. Check every detail, tick the declaration, and submit. Your complaint is verified first, and then registered. Save the acknowledgement and your complaint number. Now, what happens next? The Ombudsman hears both sides, you and the company. The hearing can be in person, or online by video. Many complaints end in mediation under Rule sixteen. The Ombudsman suggests a fair settlement, and if both sides agree, the case closes. In twenty twenty-four to twenty-five, about one in three health complaints that were closed, ended this way. If there is no settlement, the Ombudsman decides the case, and passes an award. You don't need fancy legal words. Explain your case simply. When did you buy the policy? Why were you in the hospital? And how much did the company cut? And how long does it take? Of the health complaints the Ombudsman closed in twenty twenty-four to twenty-five, about eight out of ten were closed within three months. And here is why this law is built for you. First. Under Rule seventeen, when the Ombudsman passes an award, it is binding on the insurance company. Wait... what if you are NOT happy with the award? You don't have to accept it. Other legal remedies, like the consumer forum, may still be open to you. And second. Under the I-R-D-A-I health master circular of twenty twenty-four, the insurance company must carry out the award within thirty days of receiving it. Ten days late, that is fifty thousand rupees. Thirty days late, one lakh fifty thousand rupees.
