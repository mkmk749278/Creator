## Block 2 · 1,774 characters · ~2:08 · lines O013–O024

First... let's clear up the single biggest confusion on the internet. When your claim is rejected, many people tell you to go and file a complaint on the Bima Bharosa portal. So you go to Bima Bharosa, upload your papers, and wait. Many people then get the same rejection letter from the insurance company again, and the complaint is closed. And you think, even the government portal could not help me. Here is why that happened. Bima Bharosa is NOT a court. It is the regulator's grievance tracking system, run by the I-R-D-A-I. Your complaint goes to the same insurance company that rejected your claim, and the regulator tracks how the company handles it. Bima Bharosa does not decide your claim, and it cannot pass an order telling the company to pay you. The body that can pass a binding award in your case... is the Insurance Ombudsman, set up under the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, twenty seventeen. Now, look at the second trap. Private claim recovery companies. Suppose your four lakh rupee claim is stuck. A recovery agency tells you, sign our agreement, and if we get your money back, give us twenty percent plus G-S-T. And here is what they may not tell you. Under Rule fourteen of the Insurance Ombudsman Rules, the complaint is made by you, the person with the grievance, or through your legal heir, nominee or assignee. The form is the same free form on the official website that you can fill in yourself. And never share your passwords or O-T-Ps with anyone. ## Block 3 · 1,351 characters · ~1:40 · lines O025–O037

Now listen carefully. You cannot jump straight to the Ombudsman on the day your claim is rejected. If you do, your complaint can be turned away at the door. Every year, many complaints are not even taken up, because one basic step was missed. Under Rule fourteen, you must follow a two-step clock. Clock step one. Complain in writing to the insurance company, best of all to its Grievance Redressal Officer, the G-R-O. Every insurer publishes its grievance officer's email on its website. Send one calm, formal email with your policy number, your claim number, and why their deduction is wrong. Keep a copy of that email and every reply. You will need them later. Under the I-R-D-A-I rules, the company should resolve your grievance within fourteen days. Clock step two. Wait for their reply, or wait one month. The moment the company rejects your complaint, or you are not happy with its reply, or one month passes with no reply at all... the door to the Ombudsman opens. From that day, you have one year to file your complaint with the Ombudsman, for compensation of up to fifty lakh rupees. That limit was raised to fifty lakh rupees in November twenty twenty-three. And remember one strict condition. The Ombudsman cannot take your complaint if the same dispute is already pending, or already decided, in any court, consumer forum, or arbitration.

## Block 4 · 3,014 characters · ~3:43 · lines O038–O064

So, how do you actually file, and what happens next? Open your browser and go to the official website, c-i-o-i-n-s dot co dot in. Choose Register Complaint, and verify your mobile number with an O-T-P. Then you choose your Ombudsman office. There are eighteen offices across India, in cities like Hyderabad, Bengaluru, Mumbai, Delhi and Kolkata. You pick the one that covers the place where you live, or the insurance company's branch that handled your policy. Before you submit, keep these documents ready. One. A copy of your insurance policy. Two. The hospital discharge summary and the itemised final bill. Three. The company's rejection or deduction letter. Four. A copy of the complaint you sent to the insurance company. And five. Your I-D and address proof, and a photograph. Each file can be a P-D-F or a J-P-G, up to ten M-B. Check every detail, tick the declaration, and submit. Your complaint is verified first, and then registered. Save the acknowledgement and your complaint number. Now, what happens next? Many complaints end in mediation under Rule sixteen. The Ombudsman suggests a fair settlement, and if both sides agree, the case closes. Last year, about one in three health complaints that were closed, ended this way. Often, once the Ombudsman's notice reaches the insurer, the company agrees to pay before any hearing takes place. If there is no settlement, there is a hearing. It can be in person, or online by video. You don't need fancy legal words. Explain your case simply. When did you buy the policy? Why were you in the hospital? And how much did the company cut? And here is why this law is built for you. First. Under Rule seventeen, when the Ombudsman passes an award, it is binding on the insurance company. Wait... what if you are NOT happy with the award? You don't have to accept it. Other legal remedies, like the consumer forum, may still be open to you. And second. Under the I-R-D-A-I health master circular of twenty twenty-four, the insurance company must carry out the award within thirty days of receiving it. If it is late, it must pay you a penalty of five thousand rupees for every day of delay, on top of any interest that is due. Ten days late, that is fifty thousand rupees. Thirty days late, one lakh fifty thousand rupees. And in December twenty twenty-four, in one case where a policyholder was made to fight for years, the Bombay High Court ordered the insurance company to pay costs, and said the money should be recovered from the officers responsible, after an inquiry. That is the power of knowing your rights. You don't need connections, and you don't need to pay twenty percent to middlemen. Be smart. Be informed. And always... be practical.
