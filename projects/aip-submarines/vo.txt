Night. The Arabian Sea. Somewhere under these waves, an Indian submarine is running out of battery.

Imagine you're in its control room. Nobody raises a voice. The captain gives one quiet order. Raise the snorkel.

A thin steel mast rises through black water. The diesel engines roar to life. And for the next few hours, you are easier to find than at almost any other time on your patrol.

You're inside a machine built to disappear. This is the most dangerous part of its life. And it has to do it every few days.

So why would anyone build a hiding machine that has to show itself?

There is a cure. You've probably heard its name. A-I-P, air independent propulsion. Online, they call it the ghost maker. Our submarines will become invisible, the headlines say.

But here's the catch. To fit it, India has to cut one of its own submarines in two.

And it gets stranger. India built this machine, with its own scientists. It has run on land. Yet the Navy picked a German design for its next submarines. So was the Navy wrong? Or was India's machine not good enough? The answer is, both of them were right. By the end of this film, you'll see why.

Start with the people whose job is to find you.

Say you're flying a P-Eight-I of the Indian Navy, hours out over the sea, hunting. You don't look for the submarine itself. You look for what it leaves behind. A mast. The thin white wake it draws on the water. A dot on your radar. The noise of a diesel engine.

So why would a submarine ever raise that mast?

Because under water, everything you depend on runs on one giant battery. The motor, the lights, the air plant, the computers. And the engines that charge your battery need air. Down there, there is none.

So how long can your battery last? A few days. The makers of India's Kalvari class say about four, and only if you move at a crawl. Then you must come up near the surface, raise your mast, and breathe for hours.

Picture your patrol as a strip of time. Every few days, a red mark. Each red mark is a few hours when you can be heard, seen, or caught on radar.

For a submarine navy, that's the whole game, right? Fewer red marks. Shorter red marks.

That's exactly what A-I-P does. It's a second engine that doesn't need air from outside. There are three main kinds in use today, and India chose the fuel cell.

And for you, the difference is huge. About four days on the battery becomes about two weeks under water. That's D-R-D-O's own figure from its land trials.

So, a ghost? Not quite.

A-I-P lets you crawl for weeks. It doesn't let you run. Push for speed, and you're back on the battery, draining it fast. And even when you're quiet, you can still be found. Active sonar can bounce sound off your hull, and a raised mast still leaves a wake.

What A-I-P really buys you is fewer red marks. Fewer moments of being exposed. At sea, that can decide whether you come home.

Then you might ask, why not just build nuclear submarines? India does build them. But they are far bigger, far more costly, and few. In the shallow waters close to our coast, a small, quiet diesel boat is still the right tool.

So India built its own A-I-P. And it built it in its own way.

Germany's fuel cell submarines carry their hydrogen locked inside metal tanks. India's carries no hydrogen gas at all. Can you guess what it carries?

A white powder. Sodium borohydride.

Add water, and the powder gives off hydrogen. Right there, inside the submarine, only as much as you need.

How much power is hiding in that powder? On paper, one spoonful, about seven grams, makes enough electricity to charge your phone. That's our own calculation. Now picture tonnes of it.

The hydrogen then meets oxygen. The oxygen travels as a liquid, far colder than ice, in thick, cold tanks. They meet inside a fuel cell whose heart is phosphoric acid. You won't see a flame. No explosion. Just a steady flow of electricity. And one more thing comes out. Water. That water flows back to the powder, to make more hydrogen.

Would you want all this inside a sealed steel tube with you? In October twenty twenty-six, India's Defence Secretary said it simply. These systems need a strict safety culture, with no compromise.

On paper, India's design has a real edge. There's no tank of hydrogen gas waiting to leak. The price is weight, and a salty waste liquid that the boat must hold, or pump out to sea.

Powder in. Power out. Under water, for weeks.

But there's a problem. A Kalvari submarine is about sixty-seven metres long. Walk through it, and you'll find every metre already full.

So where does this whole plant go?

You open the submarine like a book.

At Mazagon Dock in Mumbai, the hull is cut in two. A brand-new section is built, holding the powder, the oxygen, the fuel cells and the waste. It slides into the gap, and the two parts are joined around it. Reports say the boat comes out about ten metres longer. Can you picture ten metres? That's about four autorickshaws, end to end.

Now, would you trust a hull that has been cut open? That's why the submarine's original designer, France's Naval Group, watches over the work and signs off the design for safe fitting.

So the plan is ready, and the machine works. That's what you'd expect, right?

Here's the honest answer. On land, yes. On the eighth of March, twenty twenty-one, D-R-D-O's land prototype was proven, in its endurance mode and its maximum power mode.

But it has never been to sea.

And at sea, the rules are different. For its next six submarines, the Navy asked for an A-I-P already proven at sea. Not on shore. At sea. Reports say one company was turned down because its system had only been shown on land. Germany's fuel cell boats have been at sea for about twenty years.

So the Navy picked a German design. Does that mean India's machine is bad? No. It means nobody has proven it at sea yet.

Now think about it from both sides. Can you see the trap?

The Navy can't bet a war submarine on an engine that has never been to sea.

And the engine can't go to sea until a submarine is cut open for it.

Both are right. That's what makes it a trap.

And there's one more lock on that trap. A submarine can only be cut open in a big refit, when it leaves service for months of repair. And that comes once in several years.

In twenty twenty-five, India's first Kalvari boat was in for its refit. The A-I-P wasn't ready. The boat came out without it. The window closed.

So if you were in charge, how would you break the trap?

You'd start with one submarine. Reports say it will be I-N-S Khanderi.

On the twenty-fourth of October, twenty twenty-six, the A-I-P's energy module began its next phase of testing. That happened at L and T's heavy engineering yard in Hazira, Gujarat. Next, it will be powered up and tested to the Navy's rules. In early twenty twenty-seven, it goes to Mazagon Dock. The Defence Secretary says the fitting comes in a refit around the middle of that year, with sea trials to follow.

Designed at D-R-D-O's lab in Ambernath. Built by L and T. Fitted by Mazagon Dock. And the plan is to give it to all six Kalvari boats, one refit at a time.

Meanwhile, is the sea waiting? No. China's Yuan class submarines already have A-I-P. In April twenty twenty-six, Pakistan commissioned its first Hangor class submarine, which its navy says has A-I-P too.

So everything you've heard now rests on one boat, and one set of trials.

If those trials work, India will have what no tender can turn down. Its own A-I-P, proven at sea. The trap opens, for this boat and every boat after it.
