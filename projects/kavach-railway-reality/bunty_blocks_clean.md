## Block 1 · 3,193 characters · lines V001–V020

It's two in the morning on a night train... the lights are off, the A-C is humming, and your whole family is asleep on the berths. Up front, one loco pilot is staring into the dark — watching for a signal that might be red. Then one morning there's news of a train crash, and by lunchtime your family WhatsApp group is on fire. One forward says the government never bothered with safety, and another says India already has Kavach, so crashes should be impossible now. Both forwards are playing with your fear — and both are missing what really happens on the track. Welcome to Be Practical with Kishore. Today we'll open the railway's own documents, do the braking maths together, and read the official accident inquiries. And around the eight minute mark, I'll show you what the official inquiry reports actually found — and the honest answer to the hardest question of all... could Kavach have saved Balasore, or the Kanchanjunga Express?

Let's start with what Kavach actually is — because it isn't an app, and nobody can switch it on across India overnight. Kavach is India's own Automatic Train Protection system. It watches the train's speed and the signals ahead, and if the train goes too fast or is about to pass a red signal, it warns the loco pilot... then brakes by itself if nothing changes. In March twenty twenty two, in a test in Telangana with the Railway Minister on board, Kavach stopped two engines running towards each other on the same line, about three hundred and eighty metres apart. It looks like magic on video. But Kavach is a chain of four links, and it only protects you when every link is in place. Link one sits on the track — small R-F-I-D tags fixed to the concrete sleepers, which a reader under the engine reads as it passes, so the train knows exactly where it is and on which line. People say there's one tag every kilometre. Actually, one kilometre is only the longest gap allowed — near signals, points and stations the tags crowd together, and on one stretch of about ninety six kilometres between Vadodara and Ahmedabad, the railway fixed two thousand eight hundred and seventy two of them. Link two is the station. A Kavach computer there reads the signals, the points and which tracks are occupied, straight from the station's own signalling system — called interlocking. Remember that word. Link three is the radio. Tall towers along the line carry messages between the station and the engine over a U-H-F safety radio... it's not your mobile network, and it's not five G. And link four rides inside the engine — a Kavach computer, R-F-I-D readers, wheel speed sensors, a screen for the loco pilot, and a link to the brakes. Think about that for a second — this is a system designed in India, by Indian engineers and Indian companies, for Indian tracks. The electronics are built to S-I-L four, the highest safety level in railway signalling. Some posts say that means Kavach can fail only once in a billion hours... that's only half the story, because it's a strict limit for each safety function of the electronics, not a promise that no train will ever crash. So keep this one in your pocket... Kavach can only act on the information it's given.

## Block 2 · 3,529 characters · lines V021–V038

Now, if Kavach can see the red signal, why can't the loco pilot just slam the brakes, the way you would in a car? Because steel wheels on a steel rail have very little grip, a train is enormously heavy, and when the brakes go on, the air pressure has to change all the way down the brake pipe to the last coach. Here's a real number from the railway's own training notes. A Vande Bharat running at one hundred and thirty kilometres per hour needs about six hundred and fifty metres to stop... even with its emergency brakes. At that speed, it covers about thirty six metres every single second. Now picture a loaded goods train — five thousand tonnes at seventy five kilometres per hour. Its brakes build up slowly, and simple maths with fair guesses gives more than nine hundred metres to stop. And if you push the speed from one hundred and thirty to one hundred and sixty, the braking distance doesn't grow by a little... it grows by about half. Now add a December night in north India, with thick fog on the tracks. Suppose the loco pilot can see a signal only fifty metres ahead. At one hundred and ten kilometres per hour, the train crosses those fifty metres in less than two seconds — and even an alert person needs a second or two just to react. On eyes alone, the maths simply doesn't work. This is where Kavach changes the game. It doesn't wait for anyone to see the red signal — it already knows how far away it is, shows it on the screen inside the cab, and brakes early while there's still enough room. But it can't cancel physics. If danger appears closer than the stopping distance, even Kavach can't stop the train in time. And how often does a train pass a red signal by mistake? The Commission of Railway Safety noted two hundred and eight such cases in five years, from April twenty nineteen to March twenty twenty four... and twelve of them ended in a collision.

So why isn't it on every track yet? Look at the size of the job. Indian Railways runs about sixty nine thousand route kilometres, and in March twenty twenty five it had about sixteen thousand diesel and electric engines. By the end of July twenty twenty six, the newest Kavach was working on two thousand six hundred and thirty three route kilometres, on parts of the Delhi Mumbai and Delhi Howrah routes — two of the busiest in the country. Add the older version on about one thousand four hundred and sixty five kilometres in South Central Railway, and Kavach is still working on well under a tenth of the network. And here's the trap in most headlines. Track fitted with equipment, seven thousand seven hundred and twenty six kilometres, engines fitted, six thousand two hundred and ninety, and track where work has only started, twenty one thousand seven hundred and ninety four kilometres... but fitted isn't working, and work started isn't protected. The money is moving too — the yearly Kavach budget rose from about one thousand one hundred crore rupees to about one thousand six hundred and seventy crore in twenty twenty five twenty six. But money alone doesn't make it work. Every station's signalling data has to be checked, every engine type wired to its own brakes, the radio has to reach every yard, and then it's all tested and retested before a single kilometre is called working. Suppliers are the other bottleneck. For years, just three Indian companies made Kavach — Medha, H-B-L and Kernex — and a newer one, Quadrant Future Tek, told the stock exchange it got permission for passenger trials only in May twenty twenty six.

## Block 3 · 3,603 characters · lines V039–V061

Now, the question I promised you at the start. Case one. Bahanaga Bazar, near Balasore in Odisha, June two, twenty twenty three. The Coromandel Express is coming in at about one hundred and twenty eight kilometres per hour, on a green signal for the main line. But the crossover ahead is set towards the loop line... where a goods train is standing. The official inquiry found lapses in signalling circuit work done at the station. Close to three hundred people lost their lives. So, would Kavach have stopped it? Here's the honest answer — nobody can say yes or no for sure. Remember interlocking? At Balasore, the signalling system itself was showing a false green, and fed that same wrong information, Kavach could have believed the line was clear too. A safety computer can't correct wrong information it trusts. Kavach does have a few checks of its own, but no public study has shown whether they would have acted in time there. So the forward saying Kavach would surely have saved Balasore, and the one saying it surely couldn't, are both going beyond the evidence. Case two. Rangapani, near New Jalpaiguri in north Bengal, June seventeen, twenty twenty four. The automatic signals had failed, so trains were moving on a paper authority, at a slow, careful speed. The inquiry found the goods train was handed the wrong form, T-A nine one two instead of T-D nine one two, with no caution order, missing walkie talkies, and training that wasn't good enough... failures of the system, not just one loco pilot. The goods train reached seventy eight kilometres per hour and hit the Kanchanjunga Express from behind at about forty. And there was no working Kavach on that section. Here, Kavach could have made a real difference. With both trains fitted and the section working, it could have held the goods train to the allowed movement and a slow speed past those failed signals. And that famous claim that Kavach always locks the speed at exactly fifteen? Fifteen is the rule loco pilots follow at failed signals — what Kavach enforces depends on its data and settings, so don't take that number as a promise. Two crashes, two different failures. At Balasore the signalling information itself was wrong, at Kanchanjunga the backup rules broke down — and Kavach is strongest against the second kind.

So what can you actually do with all this? Three things. One. When a Kavach forward lands in your family group, ask three questions before sharing it — is the number about fitted equipment or Kavach that's working, which date is it from, and was the problem a human mistake or wrong signalling data? Two. Read the real thing instead. The railway ministry posts Kavach progress in press releases on the P-I-B website, and the Commission of Railway Safety puts its inquiry findings on its own website, free to read. Three. If you see something unsafe on a train or at a station, report it to Rail Madad by calling one three nine, or through the Rail Madad app.

Kavach isn't magic, and it isn't useless. It's an Indian-built safety layer that brakes early when a human slips... as long as the engine is fitted, the track section is working, the signalling data is right, and there's enough room to stop. Real safety isn't built on slogans. It's built by engineers and track crews doing careful, boring work right, every single day — and citizens who understand that keep the system accountable.
