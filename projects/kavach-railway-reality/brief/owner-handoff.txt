CHAPTER 1: The Cab at Midnight & The 4-Layer Hardware
(bp-kavach-ch1.mp3)
Whenever a tragic train crash happens in India, the exact same screaming debate breaks out
on the internet.​
​
One side shouts that the government is reckless and failed to install safety technology. The
other side claims that India's indigenous system, Kavach, is already a magic shield that makes
collisions completely impossible.​
​
[sigh] Both sides are playing with your emotions. But neither side understands the actual
engineering.​
​
What really happens inside a locomotive cab at two o'clock in the morning? What does Kavach
actually do? Why can't a train just hit the brakes like a car? And why would Kavach have
completely stopped the Kanchanjunga crash, but been completely blind at Balasore?​
​
Hi everyone, welcome to Be Practical with Kishore.​
​
In this video, we are not going to shout, and we are not taking political sides. Today, let’s look at
the official R-D-S-O engineering blueprints, the cold physics of train stopping distances, and the
honest truth about India's sixty-eight thousand kilometer railway rollout.​
​
Let’s break it down... in plain, simple English.​
​
First, let's clear up the biggest misconception online.​
​
Kavach is NOT a software update, and it is NOT a simple G-P-S phone app that you can turn on
overnight across the country.​
​
Kavach is a heavy, physical Automatic Train Protection system. It is certified to CENELEC
Safety Integrity Level Four—meaning the mathematical probability of a dangerous electronic
failure is less than one in a billion operating hours.​
​
To protect just one single railway line, you must physically install four separate layers of
hardware.​
​
Layer One: Trackside R-F-I-D tags. These are heavy, weatherproof yellow and black sensors
clamped directly onto concrete track sleepers every one kilometer, and placed at every signal
and track junction. They need zero electricity—they are powered wirelessly by electromagnetic
induction when a train rolls over them at one hundred and sixty kilometers per hour.​
​
Layer Two: Forty-meter steel radio towers erected every two to three kilometers along the
tracks, linked to an optical fibre cable network buried deep under the track bed.​
​
Layer Three: Station Kavach computers, housed in a double-locked room at every station, wired
directly into the station's Electronic Interlocking to read every red, yellow, and green signal.​
​
And Layer Four: Loco Kavach. Inside the locomotive cab, a dual vital computer is connected to
an under-chassis R-F-I-D reader, Doppler radar speed sensors, an in-cab display screen, and
an electro-pneumatic Brake Interface Unit plumbed directly into the train's air-brake pipes.​
​
If the driver exceeds the speed limit or approaches a red signal too fast, the computer doesn't
just ring a buzzer... it physically takes over the brakes and stops the train automatically.​


CHAPTER 2: The Terrifying Physics of Braking & Winter Fog
(bp-kavach-ch2.mp3)
Now, let’s talk about cold, hard physics.​
​
Many people ask: "Kishore, if a driver sees a red light or an obstacle ahead, why can't he just
slam on the brakes?"​
​
Here is the terrifying physical reality of railways: Metal wheels rolling on smooth steel tracks
have an extremely low friction coefficient—less than one-third of rubber tires on road asphalt.​
​
A standard twenty-four-coach passenger express hauled by a W-A-P-seven electric locomotive
weighs around fourteen hundred tonnes and stretches over six hundred meters long.​
​
When the driver hits the emergency brake, compressed air doesn't vanish instantly. An acoustic
air wave has to travel down that six-hundred-meter pipe at the speed of sound.​
​
At one hundred and ten kilometers per hour, that train requires over six hundred and eighty
meters to come to a complete stop under manual human braking.​
​
At one hundred and thirty kilometers per hour, the emergency braking distance expands to nine
hundred and eighteen meters!​
​
Under automatic Kavach intervention, because electronic sensors react in zero point two
seconds instead of human delay, that stopping distance drops to eight hundred and thirty-five
meters—saving more than eighty-three meters of stopping margin.​
​
Now look at a five-thousand-tonne loaded freight train traveling at seventy-five kilometers per
hour.​
​
If a freight train brakes too suddenly from the front, the massive momentum of fifty-eight loaded
iron-ore wagons behind it can crush couplers and cause violent derailments. So freight valves
are deliberately engineered to build brake pressure slowly over fifteen to twenty-five seconds.​
​
The result? A five-thousand-tonne freight train on level track takes nearly one whole
kilometer—over nine hundred and sixty meters—to come to a halt!​
​
[pause] Now, combine those physics with Indian winter weather.​
​
Every year in December and January, across northern and eastern India, dense radiation fog
blankets railway tracks, reducing optical visibility to less than fifty meters.​
​
A train traveling at one hundred and ten kilometers per hour covers more than thirty meters
every single second.​
​
That means in thick fog, a driver crosses that fifty-meter visibility zone in just one point six
seconds!​
​
A human being’s natural cognitive reaction time—to see a red light, register it, and pull the brake
handle—is two point five seconds.​
​
By the time the human eye physically spots a red signal lamp in heavy fog, the train has already
passed it. Mathematically, avoiding a collision based on human eyesight alone is impossible.​
​
That is why cab signaling—having the signal aspect and target stopping distance displayed
directly on a dashboard screen inside the cabin—is the only real answer.​


CHAPTER 3: The 68,000-KM Rollout Reality (bp-kavach-ch3.mp3)
So if the system is so vital, why isn’t it on every track in India right now?​
​
Did the government refuse to fund it? Or was the project neglected?​
​
[pause] Let’s look at the documented numbers.​
​
The issue is not budget. The issue is industrial capacity.​
​
Indian Railways is one of the largest rail networks on planet earth, spanning sixty-eight
thousand five hundred and eighty-four route kilometers, and over one hundred and six thousand
running track kilometers, with more than fifteen thousand active locomotives.​
​
For six years, between twenty eighteen and twenty twenty-four, only three domestic companies
in the entire country were certified by the government to manufacture Kavach equipment:
Medha Servo Drives, H-B-L Engineering, and Kernex Microsystems.​
​
Collectively, their combined factory capacity could only produce and install equipment for about
fifteen hundred route kilometers per year!​
​
If India had continued at that pace, fully equipping the network would have taken more than forty
years.​
​
Furthermore, large-scale trackside tenders were deliberately held back until July sixteenth,
twenty twenty-four. Why? Because Version Three point Two was a regional pilot that struggled
with complex multi-track railway yards and lacked interoperability.​
​
On July sixteenth, twenty twenty-four, R-D-S-O officially approved Kavach Version Four point
Zero.​
​
Version Four point Zero introduced direct digital integration with Electronic Interlocking,
improved yard route tracking, and certified high-speed braking curves for one hundred and sixty
kilometers per hour.​
​
The moment Version Four point Zero was approved, the procurement floodgates opened.​
​
In December twenty twenty-four, Chittaranjan Locomotive Works issued a massive
seven-thousand-two-hundred-crore purchase order for ten thousand onboard locomotive units.​
​
To fulfill this order, the manufacturing base was expanded from three companies to five certified
players, bringing in Quadrant Future Tek and G-G Tronics, alongside infrastructure partnerships
with Siemens and Bharat Heavy Electricals.​
​
As of mid twenty twenty-six, operational Kavach coverage stands at over four thousand route
kilometers, with active installation underway across twenty-one thousand route kilometers on
high-density corridors like Delhi–Mumbai and Delhi–Howrah, and over six thousand locomotives
equipped.​
​
And here is the economic reality: Deploying the European train protection system, E-T-C-S
Level Two, costs between one point five and three crore rupees per kilometer.​
​
Kavach costs approximately fifty lakh rupees per kilometer—about one-fourth the cost.
Developing our own technology saved India tens of thousands of crores of public money, but
building that domestic manufacturing capacity from scratch took time.​


CHAPTER 4: Disaster Forensics: Balasore vs. Kanchanjunga
(bp-kavach-ch4.mp3)
Finally... let’s answer the two hardest questions every passenger wants to know.​
​
Would Kavach have prevented the horrific Balasore crash in June twenty twenty-three?​
​
And would it have prevented the Kanchanjunga Express collision in June twenty twenty-four?​
​
Let’s look at the forensic evidence.​
​
Case Number One: Balasore.​
​
[serious tone] The honest, technical truth is: Even if Kavach Version Four point Zero had been
installed at Bahanaga Bazar, it would NOT have prevented that crash.​
​
Here is why: An Automatic Train Protection system acts on signal status, but it relies on the
station's Electronic Interlocking for ground truth.​
​
At Balasore, maintenance technicians were repairing an electric barrier gate. Because of
unapproved wiring changes, a false positive electric feed was bridged directly into the point
detection relay—seventeen N-W-K-R.​
​
The physical track switch was locked to the loop line where a freight train was parked. But the
computer was fooled into believing the switch was set straight for the main line.​
​
So the signaling system displayed a bright Green signal for a one-hundred-and-thirty kilometer
per hour run-through.​
​
Because the station computer itself believed the line was clear, it would have sent that exact
same false Green authority to Kavach! The Coromandel Express would have entered the loop
line at full speed, because the safety system was fed corrupted information.​
​
Kavach cannot detect a misplaced track point if the interlocking system itself reports that
everything is safe.​
​
Now, look at Case Number Two: The Kanchanjunga Express crash in June twenty twenty-four.​
​
In this case... Kavach would have prevented the disaster one hundred percent!​
​
At Rangapani, the automatic block signaling failed. The Station Master issued a paper authority
form, and the trailing container freight train entered the section in heavy rain. The driver violated
the mandatory fifteen-kilometer-per-hour caution rule, spotted the passenger train too late, and
rammed it from behind.​
​
Had Kavach been operational, two automatic shields would have triggered:​
​
First: To pass a defective red signal under paper authority, a driver must press the Override
button on the cab screen. The moment Override is pressed, Kavach electronically locks the
locomotive’s speed to exactly fifteen kilometers per hour. If the driver tried to speed up, the
system would automatically apply the emergency brakes.​
​
And second: Both locomotives were on the exact same track, reading the exact same sleeper
R-F-I-D tags. Every single second, Kavach locomotives broadcast their position directly to each
other over a dedicated radio frequency.​
​
The trailing freight train would have detected the passenger train ahead and slammed on the
emergency brakes automatically, more than one thousand meters before impact!​
​
[slight smile] That is the real distinction every citizen must understand.​
​
Kavach is not magic. It is an extraordinary, life-saving piece of precision engineering. It
completely eliminates human speed errors, red-signal violations, and rear-end collisions. But it
must be matched with strict maintenance discipline in our signaling rooms.​
​
Be smart. Be informed. And always... be practical.​
