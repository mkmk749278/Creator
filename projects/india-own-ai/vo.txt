But first, you need to know what's actually inside one of these machines. Because it isn't magic. It's three things.
Picture a child who has read every book in a giant library. You start a sentence... and the child guesses your next word. Then the next one. Then the next.
That's really all a chatbot does. It guesses the next piece, again and again, so well that you feel it's thinking.
So if you want to build one, you need three things. A library to read. A reading room where it can read. And teachers who know how to train the child.
The library is words — trillions of pieces of text, like the ones you type. The reading room is chips, thousands of them, running day and night for months. And the teachers? They're engineers.
India has the teachers. You know this already. Indian engineers write software for companies all over the world.
So the missing pieces are the other two. The library... and the reading room. Let's start with the library, because this is the part almost nobody tells you about.
A lot of A.I. learns from the internet. One of the biggest free copies of the web is called Common Crawl. Many A.I. models learned from it, including an early Chat G.P.T.

In January twenty twenty-five, India found out how fast that door can close.
Just days before leaving office, America's government put out a new rule. It sorted the world into three groups. Its closest allies could buy these chips freely. China was blocked.

Four months later, the new American government scrapped that rule. But India had seen it. The tap is in someone else's hand.
So what would you do? India didn't wait to own a giant reading room. It rented one... and shared it.
Under the IndiaAI Mission, private companies put their chips into one common pool. Indian startups and researchers rent time on them, and the government pays part of the bill.
