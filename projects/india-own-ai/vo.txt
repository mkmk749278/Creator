But first, you need to know what's actually inside one of these machines. Because it isn't magic. It's three things.
Picture a child who has read every book in a giant library. You start a sentence... and the child guesses your next word. Then the next one. Then the next.
That's really all a chatbot does. It guesses the next piece, again and again, so well that you feel it's thinking.
So if you want to build one, you need three things. A library to read. A reading room where it can read. And teachers who know how to train the child.
The library is words — trillions of pieces of text, like the ones you type. The reading room is chips, thousands of them, running day and night for months. And the teachers? They're engineers.
India has the teachers. You know this already. Stanford University's A.I. report puts India near the very top of the world for how common A.I. skills are.
So the missing pieces are the other two. The library... and the reading room. Let's start with the library, because this is the part almost nobody tells you about.
A lot of A.I. learns from the internet. One of the biggest free copies of the web is called Common Crawl. The makers of Chat G.P.T. trained an early model on it.
Every month or so, it saves a fresh copy — more than two billion web pages. So we took the latest one, from October twenty twenty-six. And we counted the languages for you.

And that's why your Telugu question was so expensive. Remember the chopper? It learns its pieces from this same library.

So here's the rule. A machine learns from what it reads.
And the ruin? If your language is a few pages in ten thousand, the machine learns you last... and charges you more.
The reality is, someone has to build the Indian part of the library. Page by page, voice by voice. And that's slow, expensive work.

But words are only half of it. Even with a perfect library, you need a place to read it. And this is where India hits a wall.
This is the reading room. A chip like the one you're looking at, made by an American company called Nvidia. It's called a G.P.U., and a single one costs lakhs of rupees.
Now, if you want to train a big A.I., you don't need one. You need thousands of them, wired together, running for months.
And here's the catch. India doesn't make these chips. They're designed in America and made mostly in Taiwan. If you watched our chip film, you know why.
In January twenty twenty-five, India found out how fast that door can close.

So what would you do? India didn't wait to own a giant reading room. It rented one... and shared it.
Here's how it works for you, if you're a builder. Under the IndiaAI Mission, private companies put their chips into one common pool. Indian startups and researchers rent time on them, and the government pays part of the bill.
