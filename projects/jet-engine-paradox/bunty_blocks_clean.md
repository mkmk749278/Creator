## Block 1 · 4,307 characters · lines J001–J045

August twenty three, twenty twenty three. India lands a spacecraft near the south pole of the Moon. No country had ever landed there before. India builds its own nuclear submarines. It reached Mars on its very first try. And yet... in hangars in Bengaluru and Nashik, brand new fighter jets are waiting. Painted. Radars fitted. Almost ready. But not one of these new jets has joined the Air Force yet. The biggest reason? They are waiting for an engine. An engine made in Lynn, Massachusetts, about thirteen thousand kilometres away. So here is the question. India landed on the Moon. Why can it not build its own fighter jet engine? Welcome to Be Practical with Kishore. Today, the story of one of the hardest machines on Earth to build. Single crystal metal, broken promises, and a forty year fight to join one of the world's most closed clubs.

To understand why one engine matters so much, look at the Indian Air Force. The Air Force is supposed to have forty two fighter squadrons. Today it has about twenty nine. Old jets like the MiG twenty one have retired. New jets are needed, fast. So in twenty twenty one, the government ordered eighty three Tejas Mark One A jets from Hindustan Aeronautics, HAL. In twenty twenty five, it ordered ninety seven more. But HAL does not make the engine. Every one of these jets uses the F four zero four, made by GE Aerospace in America. The engines were due from twenty twenty three. The first one came only in March twenty twenty five. GE blamed problems in its supply chain. HAL put penalties on GE for the delay. By October twenty twenty six, GE had sent only fourteen of the ninety nine engines. HAL says close to thirty jets are built and waiting. Some testing work on radar and weapons is also still pending. But the engine has been the biggest hold up. And a delay is only the small problem. The real danger is control. India has been here before. In May nineteen ninety eight, India tested nuclear weapons at Pokhran. America answered with sanctions under a law called the Glenn Amendment. Sales of the same GE F four zero four engines to India were stopped. American help on the Tejas flight controls also ended. Indian engineers had to build the flight control system on their own. They did it. The lesson was simple. If you do not own the engine, you do not fully own your fighter jet. Nine countries have nuclear bombs. About ten have put a satellite in space with their own rocket. But only a handful can design and build a modern fighter engine on their own. The main ones are America, Russia, Britain and France. And, after decades of struggle, China. India tried to join this club. And that started one of the hardest engineering stories in its history.

In nineteen eighty six, Indian engineers began planning their own fighter engine. The job went to the Gas Turbine Research Establishment, GTRE, a DRDO lab in Bengaluru. The engine was named Kaveri. Full development was approved in March nineteen eighty nine, with a budget of about three hundred and eighty two crore rupees. The plan was to finish by December nineteen ninety six. The goal was huge. An engine that gives eighty one kilonewtons of push with the afterburner on. And India was building a brand new jet, the Tejas, at the same time. Both almost from scratch. That doubled the risk. When the Kaveri prototypes reached the test bench, the numbers did not add up. It was meant to weigh eleven hundred kilos. It weighed twelve hundred and thirty five. It was meant to push eighty one kilonewtons. As reported, it reached only about seventy. And India had no facility to test a fighter engine in high altitude conditions. So Indian engineers had to take the Kaveri to Russia for those tests. The years went by. The Air Force could not wait for ever. In September two thousand eight, the Kaveri was officially separated from the Tejas programme. The Tejas would fly with the American engine. But GTRE did not stop. In twenty ten, a Kaveri flew for the first time, fitted to a Russian Ilyushin transport plane used as a flying test bed. Twenty seven flights. Up to twelve kilometres high. By twenty twelve, about two thousand crore rupees had been spent. The cost estimate had grown to over twenty eight hundred crore. So why did it fail? To answer that, we need to go inside the engine.

## Block 2 · 4,177 characters · lines J046–J090

First, a fair question. If ISRO can build rocket engines, why is a jet engine so much harder? A rocket engine fires for minutes, not hours. It carries its own oxygen. Then its job is done, and that stage falls away. A fighter jet engine must breathe air. Humid sea air. Freezing mountain air. Desert dust. It must go from idle to full power in seconds, again and again, during hard turns. And it must run for thousands of hours between overhauls. At the heart of it all is one small part. The high pressure turbine blade. It sits right behind the combustion chamber, where burning fuel makes a storm of hot gas. Now here is the shocking part. Rolls-Royce says its blades work in gas up to two hundred degrees hotter than the melting point of the metal they are made of. The gas is hotter than the metal's melting point. And still the blade does not melt. How? The first secret is in the metal itself. Normal metal is made of many tiny crystals. The borders between them are weak spots. Under heat and huge force, the metal slowly stretches along those borders. Engineers call it creep. So the best blades are grown as one single crystal. No borders at all. The metal is a nickel super alloy, with rare elements like rhenium mixed in. The second secret is air. Each blade is hollow, with tiny channels inside. Cooler air from the compressor is pushed through them. It comes out through rows of tiny holes, and covers the blade in a thin blanket of cooler air. And on top, a ceramic coating acts like a heat shield. Now add the spin. The disc holding these blades turns more than twelve thousand times every minute. Rolls-Royce says the pull on each blade is like the weight of a London bus hanging from it. Gas above the metal's melting point. A bus hanging from every blade. For thousands of hours. This is why so few countries can do it. You cannot just copy the drawing. The real knowledge is in how the metal is made, cast, drilled and tested. Countries learned it over decades of testing, and many failures. That knowledge is a closely guarded secret. For decades, no engine maker shared it with India.

But the Kaveri story did not end in two thousand eight. GTRE took out the afterburner and made a simpler version. The Dry Kaveri. In tests in Russia, it gave about forty eight kilonewtons of push, a little more than its target. It is now chosen to power India's stealth combat drones, like the planned Ghatak. Meanwhile, DRDO's metals lab in Hyderabad, DMRL, has learned to make single crystal turbine blades. First for a helicopter engine. Those years of struggle were not wasted. They built the base India did not have in nineteen eighty six. But the next big fighter, the AMCA, needs much more. The first AMCA jets will use GE's F four one four engine. For later jets, India wants a new engine of about one hundred and twenty kilonewtons. And this time, it wants to own the design. In twenty twenty five, the government picked France's Safran to build it together with GTRE. India's condition is clear. The design and the rights must belong to India. As of now, that deal is still waiting for final approval. And in August twenty twenty six, Rolls-Royce and Reliance also offered to build an engine in India. China walked this same road. Its J twenty stealth fighter used Russian engines at first. It took China decades, and many failures, to switch to engines of its own.

So let us come back to our question. Reaching the Moon needs rocket engines that work perfectly, but only for minutes at a time. A fighter engine needs metal that lives in gas hotter than its own melting point, spinning, for thousands of hours. That is the jet engine paradox. The Moon was the easier problem. For forty years, India has been learning the hard way. Today, the Dry Kaveri is heading for certification, the blade makers in Hyderabad are learning, and a new engine is on the drawing board.
