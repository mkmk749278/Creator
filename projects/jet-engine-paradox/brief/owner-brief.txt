* **Narration:** ​
  In 1986, India decided it would never be held hostage by foreign
aerospace suppliers again. The Gas Turbine Research Establishment—or
G-T-R-E—launched Project Kaveri. The objective was daring: Design an
entirely indigenous, eighty-one kilonewton afterburning bypass
turbofan engine specifically tailored for India’s high-temperature,
dusty operational climate. But India’s aerospace establishment made a
fatal planning assumption. They attempted to develop a clean-sheet
fifth-generation engine simultaneously with a clean-sheet fighter jet
airframe. In global aerospace, that is considered a cardinal sin. If
the airframe has handling problems, you blame the engine. If the
engine stalls, you blame the airframe aerodynamics. When the first
Kaveri prototypes hit the test benches, the physics proved
unforgiving. The engine was supposed to weigh eleven hundred
kilograms; it weighed over twelve hundred and thirty-five. The Indian
Air Force demanded eighty-one to ninety kilonewtons of thrust; the
Kaveri could barely squeeze out seventy kilonewtons. Worse, when
pushed to its limits, the engine suffered from violent compressor
stalls: sudden disruptions of air intake that can extinguish the flame
inside a combustion chamber in mid-flight. And India had a humiliating
structural roadblock: It did not own a High-Altitude Test Facility. To
test whether an engine can reignite at forty-five thousand feet in
thin air, Indian engineers had to box the Kaveri into shipping
containers, fly it to the Gromov Flight Research Institute in Russia,
and strap it to the wing of a modified Russian Il-76 transport plane.
By September 2008, after twenty-two years of struggle and over two
thousand crore rupees spent, the Indian Air Force could wait no
longer. The Kaveri was officially delinked from the Tejas. The dream
was shelved.​

* **Narration:** ​
  Why did India master rocket science while failing at jet engines?
Consider the operating profile. An I-S-R-O rocket engine burns
intensely for three to six minutes. It flies in one direction, carries
its own liquid oxygen, and once its job is done, it falls into the
sea. A fighter jet engine is an atmospheric vacuum cleaner operating
in hell. It must suck in humid coastal monsoon air, sub-zero Himalayan
clouds, and abrasive Rajasthani desert dust. It must throttle from
idle to afterburner in three seconds, pull 9-G vertical turns without
starving of fuel, and survive for four thousand hours before
undergoing an overhaul. And at the center of this engine lies the
single most difficult manufacturing process known to humanity: The
High-Pressure Turbine Blade. Inside the combustor, burning jet fuel
generates temperatures exceeding sixteen hundred and fifty degrees
Celsius. The nickel-based superalloys used to forge the turbine blades
melt at fourteen hundred and fifty-five degrees. Read that again: The
turbine operates at temperatures higher than the melting point of the
metal itself. If you forged that blade out of standard metal, the
boundary grains between the microscopic crystals would slide apart
under thermal creep, shattering the engine into scrap metal within
seconds. To prevent this, the entire blade must be cast as a Single
Crystal: an unbroken atomic grid where billions of nickel and chromium
atoms align in flawless crystallographic symmetry without a single
grain boundary. How does a single-crystal blade survive this inferno?
Engineers hollow out the blade with labyrinthine serpentine cooling
channels narrower than a needle. Relatively cooler air from the
compressor is pumped through these channels and bled out through
microscopic laser-drilled holes on the blade surface, creating an
invisible aerothermal film of cold air just millimeters thick. While
spinning at twenty thousand revolutions per minute, each blade
experiences centrifugal forces exceeding twenty thousand times
gravity. The physical stress is equivalent to hanging an entire
elephant from the handle of a soup spoon. Miss the chemical
composition of rhenium by zero point one percent, and the blade warps.
Drill the laser cooling hole one micron off angle, and the thermal
film collapses, melting the turbine in mid-air. You cannot master this
with mathematical software. You can only master it by blowing up
hundreds of engines over decades until you gather the proprietary
empirical data.​

foundation India lacked in 1986. But for India’s crown jewel—the

Conquering space requires mastering Newtonian physics and cryogenic
