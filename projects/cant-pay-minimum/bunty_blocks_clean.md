## Block 1 · 3,551 characters · lines C001–C035

A message comes on your phone. Your credit card payment is overdue. Then another message. Then a call from a number you don't know. In our last video, we saw how paying only the minimum keeps you in debt for years. Today, a harder question. What if you cannot pay even that minimum? Maybe your salary stopped. Maybe a hospital bill came first. It happens to good, honest people. And then the fear starts. Will the police come home? Will they take my salary? Will my family find out? Most people think there is one answer for everyone. There isn't. Someone who owes ten thousand rupees and someone who owes ten lakh live two very different stories. Today I will show you both. The rules on paper, and the reality on the ground. Welcome to Be Practical with Kishore.

First, let's understand the clock. The day your last date passes, the bank starts counting. Thirty days. Sixty days. Ninety days. The bank's system checks your account every single day. If the minimum stays unpaid for ninety days after the due date, the bank must mark your card a bad loan. They call it an NPA. A non performing asset. Now here is something most people don't know. From that day, your unpaid card is also a loss for the bank. The bank must keep aside about a quarter of what you owe, from its own profit. That is why the calls get louder around this time. But listen carefully. Some people say interest stops after ninety days. It does not. The bank cannot count your unpaid interest as its income. But your bill can keep growing as per your card terms. There is a second clock too. Your CIBIL. Banks now update the credit bureaus every fifteen days. The good part is, the bank must tell you before it reports you as a defaulter. And if you have a genuine dispute, it must wait. In one case, a consumer commission held SBI Card at fault for reporting a customer to CIBIL without proper notice first. And the card itself? At SBI Card, for example, it may be blocked from the very first missed payment.

Now the part nobody tells you. A small bill grows faster than a big bill. Take two people. Ravi owes ten thousand rupees. Sunita owes one lakh. Both stop paying. Both pay the same interest. Three point seven five percent a month, plus eighteen percent GST on it. But the late fee is not a percentage. It is a fixed amount by slab. On ten thousand, the late fee is seven hundred and fifty rupees. On one lakh, it is thirteen hundred. Seven hundred and fifty on ten thousand is seven and a half percent, every month, just as a fee. Thirteen hundred on one lakh is only one point three percent. After six months of paying nothing, Ravi's ten thousand becomes about nineteen thousand. Almost double. Sunita's one lakh becomes about one lakh thirty eight thousand. For Ravi, two thirds of that jump is only late fees. So the smaller your bill, the harder the fees hit. This is our own calculation, using one bank's published fees. Your bill will be different, but the shape is the same.

Now the real question. Who actually comes after you? And will anyone come to your house? In the first three months, it is mostly calls. Automated calls, SMS, WhatsApp. Many from big call centres in cities. Banks decide how hard to chase by two things. The days you are late, and the money at risk. After that, many banks hand your account to a recovery agency. And here is the business behind it. Many agencies earn only when they collect. A percentage of what you pay. One public sector bank pays its agencies between seven and a half and fifteen percent on accounts up to one lakh.

Now see it from their side. If you owe ten thousand, the agency may earn about a thousand rupees. And only if you pay in full. Now think about where you live. Say your card has your old room address in the city. But your family lives in a village, far away. Agencies usually work city by city, or district by district. In Andhra and Telangana, one card company's agency list names only Hyderabad, Warangal, Karimnagar, Vizag and Vijayawada. For a small amount, a long trip to a far village rarely pays for itself. So small dues mostly stay as calls and messages. That's not a rule. It's simple business. For bigger amounts, it changes. Finding a person who moved is a paid job. One bank pays seven thousand five hundred rupees for each person traced. That's worth it only when the amount is big.

What about court? Here again, the amount decides. To file a case, the bank pays court fees and a lawyer. On a small amount, that eats a big part of what it can recover. So a court case for ten thousand rarely makes sense for the bank. For bigger dues, card companies do use Lok Adalat. You get a notice for a settlement meeting, usually in your own city. In one real case, SBI Card took about one point eight lakh of card dues to a Lok Adalat in the customer's city, Indore. If you get such a notice, go. It is a chance to settle with a written record. Some banks use arbitration. Be careful here. In the Delhi High Court, one bank admitted about seventy six thousand awards where the bank alone picked the arbitrator. The court said such an award is void and cannot be enforced. The bank can still try to recover in the proper way. And the special bank tribunal, the DRT, is only for dues of twenty lakh rupees or more. For most card dues, it is not even possible. So here is my practical reading, slab by slab. No bank publishes this. Around ten thousand. Calls and messages, a blocked card and a bad mark on your CIBIL. A court case is unlikely, but the mark stays with you for years. Fifty thousand to one lakh. Calls, then an agency, maybe a visit if you live in a city they cover, and settlement or Lok Adalat notices. Two to five lakh. Expect field visits, people tracing you if you moved, and legal notices. Ten lakh and above. A court case becomes worth the bank's money. Take it seriously from day one. Remember, these slabs are about what you owe to one bank. Other banks don't add it up. They only see it on your CIBIL.

Now, two traps that catch salaried people. Trap one. Your salary account is in the same bank as your card. Your card terms allow that bank to hold or take money from your account to cover what you owe. It is called the right of set off. The national consumer commission upheld one bank taking fifty three thousand rupees from a savings account for card dues. And in February twenty twenty six, in a loan case, the Kerala High Court said a bank's hold is not a court attachment, so the usual salary protection doesn't stop it. But it is not unlimited. Only your money, only for your debt, only in that bank, and only up to what you owe. A bank where you owe nothing cannot touch your account. And the bank does not have to warn you first. Some banks only promise to tell you within three days after the hold. Trap two. Many products in one bank. RBI rules mark the borrower, not just one account. So if your card at a bank becomes an NPA, your other loans at that same bank can be marked NPA too. To clean it, you must clear the overdue on all of them. And cards from different banks? They don't share your accounts. But they all see your CIBIL. One default can make the others cut your limit. That's why paying one card with another is so dangerous. Some banks can even cancel a card used that way. When one card stops, the whole chain falls.

## Block 3 · 3,273 characters · lines C063–C085

Now the biggest fear. Will the police arrest you? ... Here is the truth. Simply not being able to pay is not a crime. The Supreme Court has said that not repaying a loan is not cheating, unless you planned to cheat from the very start. And in twenty twenty five, it said police and FIRs are not a way to recover money. But a few things can become a criminal case. A cheque that bounces. And an auto debit that fails because there is no money. And of course, lying when you applied. So never give a cheque you know will bounce. And if your auto debit will fail, talk to the bank before the date.

One more myth. After six months, the bank writes it off. So it's gone, right? No. A write off is the bank cleaning its own books. Your debt is not forgiven. SBI Card, for example, writes off an account after more than six missed payments in a row. But you still owe it. And some old bad loans are sold to recovery companies for a small part of their value. Then they call you.

So what should you actually do? Five practical steps. Step one. Don't hide. Call the bank before day ninety, and also write to them. Tell them what happened and what you can pay. Keep every message. Step two. Know the recovery rules. Calls only between eight in the morning and seven in the evening. No threats, no abuse, no calls to shame your family or friends. Anyone who comes home must carry an ID card and the bank's authorisation letter. Ask to see them. If they break these rules, complain to the bank first. No answer in thirty days? Go to the RBI Ombudsman on the RBI complaint website. It is free. But since July twenty twenty six, you have only ninety days to do it. The RBI helpline is fourteen four four eight. If someone threatens you, call one one two. If you get a fake link or a fake notice, call one nine three zero. Step three. Check every legal notice yourself. A lawyer's notice is not a court summons. A real court case has a case number. Type it yourself on the eCourts website. Never tap the link in the message. Step four. If you settle, settle on paper. Get the settlement letter from the bank first. Pay only through the bank's own channel. Never cash to an agent. And know the cost. Your CIBIL will say settled, not closed. That bank won't lend to you for at least a year. Later, ask the bank what you must pay to clean that record. Step five. If you have many cards, plan the order. This is my practical view, not a rule. First, the bank where your salary sits, because that bank can touch your account. Next, the small bills, because fees grow them fastest. Then the rest. You can choose where your salary goes. But the debt doesn't go away. Use the breathing space to settle, not to run.

So, can they arrest you? Not just for being unable to pay. Can they take your salary? Without a court order, only that same bank, and only up to what you owe. Will they come home? That depends on how much you owe, and where you live. The fear is real. But fear is also their tool. Knowledge is yours. Tell me in the comments. Has a recovery agent ever called you or your family?
