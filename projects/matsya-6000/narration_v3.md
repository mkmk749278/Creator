## The narration, as it will be voiced (`vo_v3.txt`)

1. Six kilometres under the sea, the water will squeeze this titanium ball — with almost one lakh tonnes.

2. Now imagine you're one of the three Indians sitting inside it. This ball is the heart of Matsya, India's first deep-sea sub for people.

3. And between you and the sea, there are seven welds. If even one of them is weak, nothing else matters.

4. Now here's the strange part. The hardest of those welds was made by Isro. Yes, the rocket people. Isro's hardest weld wasn't for a rocket. It was for this ball.

5. Their machine could weld two centimetres of titanium. This wall is eight. It took them about seven hundred tries.

6. So how did they do it? And after Titan, how will anyone know it holds? Stay with me, and you'll know both. You'll also see what this ball has to do with the phone in your hand.

7. Hold out one finger, and look at the nail.

8. Every ten metres you go down, the sea presses on you as hard as all the air above you. Ten metres, once. Twenty metres, twice. Now keep going, six hundred times.

9. At six thousand metres, the water presses on that one fingernail with about six hundred kilos. That's a full-grown buffalo, standing on your fingernail.

10. Now cover the whole ball with fingernails, and put a buffalo on every one. Can you picture it? That's how you get almost one lakh tonnes, from every side at once.

11. I know what you want to ask me. Kishore, we've seen this story before. Is this another Titan?

12. Think back to June twenty twenty-three. Titan broke apart on its way down to the Titanic. Five people died.

13. Its hull was carbon fibre. After earlier dives, its layers had started to come apart, and it kept diving anyway. The U-S Coast Guard said the company's own failures caused it.

14. And one number has stayed with me. Where Titan broke, the sea was pressing a little more than half as hard as it will press on Matsya.

15. So what is India doing differently? Three things. The metal is titanium, which deep-sea subs have trusted for more than fifty years. The shape is a ball. And the builders don't get to pass their own work. An outside inspection company has to approve it. For Matsya, that company is from Norway.

16. And if something goes wrong while you're down there? The crew can drop heavy weights, and the sub rises back up. And you carry air for ninety-six hours.

17. Squeeze a cricket ball in your fist. Nothing happens, right? The push spreads all the way round. There's no corner and no flat side where the pressure can win.

18. That's why your cabin is a ball, and not a long tube like a normal submarine. Even America's famous deep-sea sub, Alvin, is built around a ball. Its first one, in nineteen sixty-four, was steel. In nineteen seventy-three, they changed it to titanium, and Alvin could go almost twice as deep.

19. India walked the same road, one step at a time. In twenty twenty-one, it lowered a test ball of plain steel six hundred metres into the Bay of Bengal. Then came titanium. And titanium brought the real problem.

20. You can't make a titanium ball this big in one piece. So how many pieces? Isro's own drawing shows five. A top hatch. Three rings. A bottom dome. Together, about five and a half tonnes of titanium.

21. Five pieces are useless until someone joins them. That takes seven welds. Four go right round the ball, like belts. Three go round its windows.

22. Now, titanium has one weakness. When it's hot, it pulls in oxygen and nitrogen from the air. The weld fills with tiny holes. It turns weak, and easy to crack.

23. So a normal flame is no use here. What would you do? Isro fired a beam of electrons instead, inside a vacuum chamber. No air, so nothing gets in.

24. But there was a catch. Isro's welding centre in Bengaluru could go two centimetres deep. This wall is eight.

25. You might say, Kishore, then buy a bigger machine. They made their own machine stronger. From fifteen kilowatts of power to forty.

26. And then they practised. How many times, before they touched the real ball? Not ten times. Not a hundred. About seven hundred trial welds, until every setting was exactly right.

27. Then came the real one, the weld your life will hang on. One of the big welds that go right round the ball. Eight centimetres deep, and about seven metres long, with no stopping halfway.

28. How long did it take? Thirty-two minutes. One pass. Isro says it was the first time anyone in India had made a weld like this.

29. Seven hundred tries, for thirty-two minutes that had to be perfect.

30. But how do you know it's good inside? You can't cut it open to check.

31. So they made their X-ray machine strong enough to see through eight centimetres of titanium. They used sound waves too, listening for hidden cracks. And then an outside agency has to certify it.

32. Now step inside with me. The wall is eight centimetres thick, so inside, you get just over two metres. Lie down, and your head and your feet would almost touch the walls.

33. You and two others sit here for a normal dive of twelve hours. Oxygen comes in. The carbon dioxide you breathe out gets cleaned away.

34. And India has already sent two of its own people into that darkness. In August twenty twenty-five, they went down in a French sub called Noh-teel. Raju Ramesh, a scientist, reached about four thousand metres.

35. The next day, Commander Jatinder Pal Singh, a retired Navy officer, reached about five thousand. Each of them spent seven hours down there.

36. Two Indians have seen the deep. But you know the catch, don't you? They saw it from someone else's sub.

37. Is Matsya ready to take them, then? Not yet. Let me be honest with you about what's still missing.

38. Your ball is only part of the sub. Around it go batteries, lights, motors and arms. Put it all together, and the whole thing sinks like a stone.

39. So it's wrapped in a special foam, full of tiny hollow glass bubbles set in hard plastic. The titanium takes the squeeze. The foam brings you home.

40. The ball was welded in India. The foam had to come from France, and it arrived late. Then, early this year, officials said a small crack was found in its cover.

41. And the sea has its own rule, as you know. You can't do this test in the monsoon, an official said. So the first test dive, to five hundred metres, had to wait.

42. On the sixth of November, Matsya finished another round of trials near Chennai, without people inside. Officials have said the full six kilometre dive could come in twenty twenty-seven.

43. Why go through all this? Take out your phone.

44. Your battery needs metals like cobalt and nickel. Now come down with me, six thousand metres. There's no sunlight at all. Your lights are the only lights for six kilometres. Outside your window, the water is close to freezing.

45. And the seafloor in front of you is covered with black lumps, like stones. What's inside them? Those lumps hold nickel, copper and cobalt.

46. India has held the right to explore a big patch of that seabed, in the middle of the Indian Ocean, since two thousand and two. In twenty twenty-two, an Indian robot sub surveyed it, more than five kilometres down.

47. On Independence Day last year, the Prime Minister used a name you grew up with, for India's turn towards the deep sea. Samudra Manthan. The churning of the ocean. And India's Deep Ocean Mission has more than four thousand crore rupees behind it.

48. But I'll be straight with you. Nobody is allowed to mine there yet. The world is still writing the rules. Matsya is not a mining machine. It's a pair of eyes.

49. And what about China? Its sub has already taken people to the deepest point on Earth, almost eleven thousand metres. So yes, India is behind, and you should know that. India's first goal is six thousand, deep enough for the seabed it wants to explore.

50. But look at how India is getting there, before you judge. Isro designed this ball. Isro welded it. And when its machine wasn't strong enough, it made the machine stronger.

51. Now, one last time, come back inside the ball.

52. Would you go down today? Matsya has not been to sea with people in it yet. And no public report says this titanium ball has been tested at full pressure yet.

53. So the last test will be the sea itself. One dive. Six kilometres. Three Indians, and seven welds.

54. Under that much water, your ball is only as strong as its weakest weld. That's why seven hundred tries were not too many.

55. So here's what you and I watch for. First, the test dive to five hundred metres. Then, the big one.

56. When Matsya goes down, we'll be there. Subscribe, so you're there too. And tell me in the comments. Is the deep sea worth this much effort for India? I read every one.

57. This is Be Practical with Kishore. Stay practical.
